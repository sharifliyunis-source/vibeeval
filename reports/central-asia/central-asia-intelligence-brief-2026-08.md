# Central Asia Monthly Intelligence Brief — August 2026

*Country scope: Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan.*

*Sourcing note: this is the inaugural edition. No user-supplied source material was available, so the analysis below was produced via independent web research covering roughly May through early August 2026, weighted toward July–August activity. From the next edition onward, this brief will primarily synthesize source material supplied directly by the requester, per the standing arrangement, with independent research as a fallback only when agreed in advance.*

## Executive Summary

- **Kazakhstan's oil-export route took a real but short-lived hit.** Ukrainian drone strikes (17–20 July) shut the Caspian Pipeline Consortium's Black Sea terminal, cutting Tengiz output by more than half before exports resumed on 27 July. The route's single-corridor dependence means this is a recurring risk, not a closed incident, for as long as the war continues.
- **Political continuity was reinforced in Kazakhstan, contested in Kyrgyzstan, and unresolved in Tajikistan.** Kazakhstan's Constitutional Court cleared President Tokayev for a further term; Kyrgyzstan is still absorbing the fallout from security chief Tashiev's dismissal and criminal charges ahead of a January 2027 election; Tajikistan's succession question around President Rahmon remains an open, analyst-flagged risk rather than a resolved one.
- **Regional connectivity is deepening on multiple tracks at once.** The Kyrgyzstan-Tajikistan border is ~90% demarcated (resolving one of the region's longest-running disputes), the five Central Asian states plus Azerbaijan signed the Cholpon-Ata Declaration on transport, energy and climate cooperation, and the China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway is now in active (if early-stage) construction — though its completion date is genuinely disputed among sources (2028–2030).
- **Uzbekistan is emerging as the region's most concrete near-term opportunity.** A new US-Uzbekistan Joint Investment Platform, a 76-project critical-minerals pipeline, and the region's strongest growth forecasts (6.8–8.1% for 2026) stand out against Turkmenistan's continued data opacity and narrower, largely China-directed foreign-investment access.
- **Sanctions-compliance risk is rising as a cross-border, not single-country, issue.** Reports (not yet officially confirmed) of a sharp rise in dual-use re-exports toward Russia via EAEU customs-free borders — with the EU's 20th sanctions package specifically targeting the Kyrgyz corridor — mean banking, trade-finance and logistics counterparties across Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan should expect heavier due-diligence scrutiny going forward.
- **The post-strike Iran shock is reinforcing the Middle Corridor's strategic weight.** Following the February 2026 US-Israeli strikes and Iran's subsequent food-export ban, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan are visibly diversifying transit away from the Iranian corridor toward China and Pakistan routes — a trend that favors Middle Corridor and CKU-railway investment cases over southern-corridor ones.

## Geopolitical Landscape

### Great-power competition

- **Russia:** Russia holds the 2026 CSTO chairmanship and is preparing a leaders' summit in Moscow on 11 November 2026, alongside technical meetings (e.g., the CSTO Interstate Commission for Military-Economic Cooperation, 18 June 2026, Minsk), per CSTO's own statements. Separately, on 25 July, President Tokayev of Kazakhstan, appearing alongside Vladimir Putin at a Russia-Kazakhstan forum in Omsk, publicly called for a freeze in the Ukraine war and a return to Istanbul-format talks; Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov rejected this the same day ("a freeze as such is impossible"), per Reuters-sourced reporting picked up by the Kyiv Independent, Euronews and Moscow Times. *Analysis:* the public daylight between Astana and Moscow on this point, while Tokayev simultaneously touted $28bn in 2025 bilateral trade, illustrates Kazakhstan's continuing balancing act rather than any rupture. Russia's Labor Ministry has also tightened 2026 migrant-worker quotas (e.g., construction quota cut from 80% to 50% foreign labor), accelerating a multi-year trend of Central Asian labor migration diversifying toward the Gulf, UK, Germany, and South Korea.
- **China:** SCO foreign ministers met in Cholpon-Ata, Kyrgyzstan on 24 July to prepare the 25th-anniversary Heads of State Summit set for Bishkek on 1 September 2026. China remains the dominant capital source for regional infrastructure: Chinese FDI into Kazakhstan's critical-minerals sector reached an estimated $2.6bn in 2025 (~18% of total inbound FDI), and the China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan (CKU) railway — now targeted for 2030 completion per President Japarov's March 2026 statement — remains the flagship connectivity project.
- **US/EU:** Following the first-ever C5+1 Presidential Summit at the White House (6 November 2025), where roughly twenty US-business deals were announced, Secretary Rubio stated an intention to visit all five Central Asian states in 2026 — a stated plan, not yet independently confirmed as executed. The EU's Global Gateway package (€12bn pledged, including €6.4bn for water/energy/climate and €3bn for transport) continues to underpin Middle Corridor connectivity financing.
- **Turkey/Organization of Turkic States:** An informal OTS summit was held in Turkistan, Kazakhstan on 15 May 2026 on AI and digital cooperation; a regular OTS summit is slated for Türkiye in fall 2026. On 31 July, the five Central Asian presidents and Azerbaijan's Ilham Aliyev signed the **Cholpon-Ata Declaration** at an informal "C5+1 Azerbaijan" consultative meeting, covering transport/logistics, energy security and climate adaptation — a further sign of institutionalizing a Caspian-Turkic connectivity axis alongside the OTS.
- **Iran:** Following the US-Israeli strikes of 28 February 2026 that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei (succeeded by his son Mojtaba Khamenei) and Iran's subsequent food-export ban, Central Asian trade with and transit through Iran has been disrupted; regional analysts describe Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan actively diversifying transit routes toward China and Pakistan rather than restoring reliance on the Iranian corridor. Effects on Gulf-state investment plans are cited by some analysts but should be treated as projections, not confirmed outcomes.
- **India:** India's Chabahar-based connectivity ambitions to Central Asia have weakened further: no budget line was allocated to Chabahar in India's 2026-27 union budget, the first such omission in roughly a decade, amid continued uncertainty over US sanctions waivers.

### Intra-regional relations

- **Kyrgyzstan-Tajikistan border:** Following the March 2025 border treaty, the two sides began physically installing boundary pillars on 14 July 2026 at the Kyrgyzstan-Tajikistan-Uzbekistan tripoint, with roughly 90% of engineering work reportedly complete and full demarcation targeted for 2027. This resolves one of the region's longest-running territorial disputes; residual risk lies in the remaining ~10% of unmarked sections.
- **Water/energy:** Tajikistan has budgeted over $1bn (10bn somoni) for Rogun dam construction in 2026 and has an agreement to export Rogun power to Uzbekistan at 3.4 cents/kWh. Some project-monitoring sites report World Bank financing delays tied to unmet conditions; this claim is less corroborated by mainstream outlets and should be treated as preliminary pending confirmation from the World Bank itself.
- **Connectivity:** At the 31 July Cholpon-Ata gathering, leaders specifically discussed the CKU railway and the Kambar-Ata-1 hydropower project. Separately, Uzbekistan's Mirziyoyev conducted a state visit to Kyrgyzstan (30 July–1 August) focused on trade, transport and border-infrastructure upgrades.

### Internal politics

- **Kazakhstan:** The Constitutional Court ruled on 7 July 2026 that Tokayev's current term does not count against new limits set by the March 2026 constitutional referendum, clearing him to seek a further term from 2029; the amendments also merged parliament's chambers and restored a vice-presidential post.
- **Kyrgyzstan:** The February 2026 dismissal of security chief Kamchybek Tashiev continues to reshape the power structure around President Japarov; Tashiev was charged in the spring with abuse of office and "violent seizure of power" allegations, though not detained. A protest ban remains in force, and the next presidential election is confirmed for January 2027 (not moved earlier).
- **Tajikistan:** President Rahmon's succession planning (widely presumed to favor son Rustam Emomali) remains unresolved and subject to competing family and elite interests — this is analysts' assessment, not an official government position. In July, Tajik security services conducted raids and deportations targeting undocumented Afghan nationals, and Germany deported a Tajik opposition activist despite documented detention risk.
- **Uzbekistan:** Relatively stable governance activity, marked by diplomatic outreach (first Uzbek state visit to Georgia in 23 years, July 2–3) and a cabinet reshuffle installing a new energy minister (27 July).
- **Turkmenistan:** Set to chair the CIS in 2026 and host the VIII Consultative Meeting of Central Asian Heads of State, alongside its 35th independence anniversary; independent verification of internal conditions remains limited given the country's restricted information environment.

### Afghanistan spillover

Regional governments continue to describe Afghanistan as a persistent rather than acute threat, per EU ISS and Crisis Group-style assessments; ISIS-K (ISKP) retains propaganda reach into Central Asian audiences. Tajikistan remains the most vocal in framing the Taliban as a direct threat and has not normalized diplomatic ties, while Turkmenistan continues functional engagement with Kabul on the TAPI pipeline (Serhetabat-Herat segment targeted for completion by end-2026) — illustrating a split regional approach between security wariness and economic pragmatism toward the Taliban government.

## Geoeconomic Landscape

### Trade corridors and connectivity

- **Middle Corridor digitalization push (April 2026):** Kazakhstan, China, Azerbaijan, Georgia and Turkey approved a 2026 work plan for the Trans-Caspian International Transport Route focused on digitalizing customs/transit processes. Most recent verified volume data are from 2024 (cargo up 63% y/y to 4.1 million tonnes; container traffic up 2.6x) — no confirmed 2026 throughput figures were found, a gap worth noting for executives tracking corridor capacity.
- **Financing:** The World Bank approved an $846 million guarantee in early 2026 backstopping $1.4 billion in financing for national rail operator Kazakhstan Temir Zholy, with Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank support.
- **China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan (CKU) railway:** The 305 km, $4.7 billion line entered active construction, with 4 of 29 tunnels underway. Completion timelines are disputed: Kyrgyz President Japarov cited 2030 in late March 2026, while TRACECA's Secretary-General said in June 2026 the line could open as early as 2028–29. Only ~5% physical completion is targeted by end-2026.
- **Sanctions-linked rerouting:** Several 2026 monitoring reports describe a roughly 400% rise in Kazakh/Kyrgyz/Uzbek re-exports of dual-use goods toward Russia via EAEU customs-free borders. This figure comes from sanctions-monitoring/advocacy sources, not official trade statistics, and should be read as indicative rather than confirmed. The EU's 2026 20th sanctions package tightened controls specifically targeting the Kyrgyzstan re-export corridor.
- **Kazakhstan-Uzbekistan trade:** Bilateral trade reached $4.8bn in 2025 and rose 37% y/y to $2.3bn in Jan–May 2026; the two governments signed ~$146m in new agreements on 24 July 2026 and are advancing 80 joint projects (~$1.8bn) under a $7.8bn Investment and Trade Roadmap, including plans to lift joint rail-corridor capacity from 35 to 60 million tonnes/year.

### Energy, uranium and critical minerals

- **Kazakhstan oil disruption:** Ukrainian drone strikes (17–20 July 2026) shut the Caspian Pipeline Consortium's Black Sea terminal (normal capacity ~1.8 million b/d). Tengiz output fell 56% (925,000 to ~406,000 b/d); combined Tengiz/Kashagan/Karachaganak output reportedly fell 70–90% vs. June per one industry source. Exports resumed 27 July. *Outlook:* recurrence risk remains while the war continues, given Kazakhstan's continued dependence on this single export route.
- **Uranium:** Kazatomprom — supplier of roughly 40% of global uranium — will cut planned 2026 output about 10% (32,777 to 29,697 tU), citing a "value over volume" strategy rather than input constraints. Long-term contract prices are described as stable near $80/lb.
- **Gas:** Turkmenistan targets 76 bcm of 2026 gas output, still overwhelmingly destined for China via the ~40 bcm/year Central Asia-China pipeline system. CNPC and Turkmengaz signed an April 2026 deal on Galkynysh field's fourth development phase plus a new 10 bcm/year processing plant. A long-discussed fourth pipeline ("Line D") remains unbuilt.
- **Nuclear:** Kazakhstan and Russia signed intergovernmental agreements (May 2026) for a Rosatom-built two-reactor VVER-1200 plant at Ulken/Balkhash (construction from 2027, first power 2034; ~$14.4bn plus $2bn for security/social infrastructure). A second plant site (Almaty region) was approved with China's CNNC as prospective lead constructor, at an earlier, less committed stage.
- **Critical minerals:** Uzbekistan outlined 76 rare-metals projects (~$2.6bn) over three years; the new US-Uzbekistan Joint Investment Platform (launched 16 June 2026) names critical minerals a priority sector. Processing/refining capacity across the region remains largely undeveloped, a point on which multiple independent analysts agree.
- **Energy security:** Kyrgyzstan remains under a declared energy-sector state of emergency (through Dec 2026) and expects ~4 bcm-equivalent of imports this year. Tajikistan reported the Nurek reservoir at a relatively low level (13 July 2026) and is curbing power exports to conserve water. Renewables continued to scale: Kazakhstan's ~$645m, 500 MW Karaganda wind project and the 1 GW Mirny wind farm; Uzbekistan's $1bn, 1.65 GW Masdar solar project in Samarkand.

### Investment, currencies and fiscal conditions

- **Uzbekistan:** $43.1bn in FDI and loans reported for 2025 (China $17.1bn largest, per one source not independently cross-checked). IMF raised its 2026 growth forecast to 6.8%, while Tashkent's own July 2026 forecast is a more optimistic 8.1%. Fiscal deficit targeted at 3% of GDP; inflation targeted at 7% (from 7.8% in Nov 2025).
- **Kazakhstan:** Samruk-Kazyna held $88.6bn in assets as of 31 Dec 2025, 96% invested domestically; government targets $25.5bn/year in FDI and ~€54bn in fixed capital over 2026–28. Inflation eased to 10.3% in June 2026 (ninth consecutive monthly decline), prompting a base-rate cut to 16.75% on 24 July. 2026 GDP growth forecasts range from 4.6% (IMF) to 5.4% (government).
- **Kyrgyzstan:** GDP reportedly grew 12.4% y/y in Jan–Apr 2026, attributed to Kambarata-1 and CKU railway construction spending — a rate well above regional peers that likely reflects construction-investment activity rather than broad-based growth (inference); the World Bank's separate 2026 forecast is a more conservative 6.1%. Inflation ran near 11% in June 2026.
- **Tajikistan:** External debt was $3.037bn as of 1 Jan 2026 (down 4.7% y/y) but remains near half of GDP; the IMF classifies both Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan at "high risk of debt distress." 2026 growth is projected near 6%.
- **Turkmenistan:** Data are comparatively opaque and largely government self-reported. Official figures cite 6.3% growth in 2025 and a planned $11.8bn 2026 investment program. The IMF completed an Article IV mission in June 2026, but a detailed public staff report was not located — flagged as a data gap rather than filled with unverified numbers.

### Regional integration: EAEU and SCO

- The Supreme Eurasian Economic Council met in Astana (29 May 2026) under Kazakhstan's 2026 EAEU chairmanship, focused on external partnerships, connectivity and digital-market integration. One outlet cited intra-bloc trade turnover exceeding $80bn in the prior year and "expected to exceed $100bn" in 2026 — a single-source figure pending confirmation from the EAEU's own statistical service. Next Council meeting: December 2026, St. Petersburg.
- The SCO's 25th-anniversary summit is scheduled for Bishkek, 31 Aug–1 Sept 2026, following a July 2026 foreign ministers' meeting reportedly preparing institutional/financial reforms to deepen the bloc's economic role. Adoption is unconfirmed and falls just after this brief's period.

### Major infrastructure projects

- **Kambarata-1** (Kyrgyzstan 34% / Kazakhstan 33% / Uzbekistan 33%): $4.2bn, ~1,860 MW hydropower project on the Naryn River. First-round talks on the intergovernmental construction agreement were held in Bishkek (31 March–2 April 2026); no final financial close confirmed.
- **Rogun** (Tajikistan): At least 8.2 billion TJS (of a planned 10bn+ TJS, >$1bn) earmarked in the 2026 budget; the World Bank approved a second financing phase including a $300m IDA grant. Two of six planned 600 MW units are operational; the third is targeted for September 2027.

## Political Risk Assessment — Threats & Opportunities for Private Sector

*Assessed for both companies already operating in the region and companies evaluating entry.*

### Kazakhstan
- **Export-logistics disruption — elevated, near-term.** The 17–20 July CPC/Tengiz strikes cut Tengiz output 56% before a 27 July resumption; single-route dependence on the CPC Black Sea terminal means recurrence risk stays elevated while the Ukraine war continues. *Operators* with oil-export exposure face live revenue/scheduling risk this quarter; *entrants* to the upstream/logistics space should price route concentration into any new commitment.
- **Sanctions/re-export compliance — moderate, rising.** EAEU customs-free borders are cited (via sanctions-monitoring sources, not official data) as a growing dual-use re-export conduit to Russia; Kazakhstan sits inside this bloc even though the EU's 20th package named the Kyrgyz corridor specifically. *Operators* in trade finance, banking and logistics face a growing compliance/reputational burden; *entrants* should assume enhanced due-diligence expectations from Western banks and counterparties.
- **Political continuity — low.** The Constitutional Court's July ruling clears Tokayev for a further term; this reduces near-term succession uncertainty relative to peers, though it is a durability-of-status-quo signal rather than a reform signal (inference).
- **Opportunity — macro/FDI window.** Easing inflation (10.3%, ninth straight monthly decline), a July rate cut, and stated $25.5bn/year FDI and €54bn fixed-capital targets, plus renewables build-out (Karaganda, Mirny wind) and Middle Corridor/KTZ financing ($846m WB guarantee), favor both *operator* expansion and *entrant* market access in renewables, logistics and critical minerals — though 96% of Samruk-Kazyna's $88.6bn book stays domestic, a reminder that state capital is not itself the entry vehicle.

**Net:** elevated export-logistics and moderate sanctions-compliance risk for anyone with oil/trade-finance exposure this quarter, against an otherwise constructive and improving macro/FDI backdrop for new entrants.

### Uzbekistan
- **Regulatory/market-access — moderate, easing.** Licensing and JV/local-partner norms remain a real entrant barrier but are moving in a liberalizing direction alongside sustained reform activity (diplomatic outreach, cabinet reshuffle).
- **Policy continuity — moderate.** The 27 July energy-minister change introduces near-term uncertainty for *operators* already active in the energy sector pending the new minister's direction; low direct relevance for *entrants* not yet committed.
- **Opportunity — clear entry window.** The US-Uzbekistan Joint Investment Platform (launched 16 June 2026) names critical minerals a priority sector, alongside a 76-project, ~$2.6bn rare-metals pipeline and largely undeveloped processing/refining capacity — a genuine first-mover opening for entrants willing to build midstream capacity, not just extract. $43.1bn in 2025 FDI/loans and IMF/government growth forecasts of 6.8–8.1% support *operator* expansion cases too.

**Net:** currently the region's most favorable entry window, especially in critical minerals and processing, tempered by moderate regulatory-transition and energy-leadership uncertainty for incumbents.

### Turkmenistan
- **Data opacity — elevated.** Restricted information flow, a completed but unpublished IMF Article IV mission, and largely self-reported figures ($11.8bn 2026 investment program, 6.3% 2025 growth) make due diligence unusually hard for *entrants*; foreign-ownership/JV structures are correspondingly harder to verify against stated policy.
- **Reputational exposure — moderate.** Continued functional engagement with the Taliban on TAPI (Serhetabat-Herat segment targeted end-2026) creates reputational proximity risk for any company partnering on that corridor.
- **Opportunity — narrow.** Gas-sector deals (CNPC/Turkmengaz Galkynysh phase 4) exist but are overwhelmingly China-directed; direct entry points for other foreign operators remain limited absent the long-discussed, unbuilt fourth pipeline.

**Net:** low transparency keeps market-access and reputational risk elevated for prospective entrants; opportunity is narrow and largely closed to non-Chinese, non-TAPI-linked operators.

### Kyrgyzstan
- **Political/security — elevated.** The Tashiev dismissal and subsequent charges, an active protest ban, and the run-up to the January 2027 election raise near-term political uncertainty for *operators*, though no direct operational disruption is yet reported.
- **Sanctions-corridor exposure — elevated, specific.** The EU's 20th package explicitly targets the Kyrgyz re-export corridor; banking, logistics and trade-finance counterparties face the most acute compliance/reputational risk in the region on this axis.
- **Fiscal — moderate.** IMF classifies Kyrgyzstan at high risk of debt distress; relevant to any counterparty relying on state guarantees or co-financing.
- **Opportunity — long-horizon logistics.** CKU railway construction (only ~5% complete by end-2026, completion disputed 2028–30) and early-stage Kambarata-1 talks offer *entrants* a genuine but early and execution-risk-laden logistics/infrastructure play; construction-driven 12.4% y/y growth (Jan–Apr) benefits *operators* in that sector specifically, likely not broad-based (inference).

**Net:** a real but early-stage logistics-corridor opportunity set against the region's most acute sanctions-corridor compliance risk and elevated near-term political uncertainty.

### Tajikistan
- **Succession uncertainty — elevated, medium-term.** Rahmon succession planning remains unresolved among competing elite interests (analysts' assessment, not official position), a standing source of medium-term political risk.
- **Afghanistan-adjacent security — elevated, localized.** Tajikistan's raids/deportations of undocumented Afghans and its comparatively hawkish Taliban stance point to continued security sensitivity near the southern border, relevant to *operators* with logistics or personnel exposure there.
- **Rule-of-law signal — moderate, reputational.** Germany's deportation of a Tajik activist despite documented detention risk is a reputational flag worth weighing for partnerships with government-adjacent counterparties.
- **Fiscal/resource — moderate.** High risk of debt distress and a low Nurek reservoir (curbing power exports) create moderate operational risk for power-dependent operators regionally, including in Uzbekistan.
- **Opportunity.** Rogun financing (>$1bn 2026 budget, WB $300m IDA grant) and the Uzbekistan power-export agreement (3.4¢/kWh) offer *entrants* in construction, engineering and cross-border power trading a concrete, MDB-backed opening; the resolved Kyrgyzstan border (90% pillared) modestly reduces cross-border logistics risk in the Ferghana Valley.

**Net:** Rogun-linked construction and power-trading opportunities are real but sit against elevated succession and debt-distress risk — best suited to entrants with MDB-backed cover or high risk tolerance.

### Cross-cutting: secondary-sanctions exposure via the EAEU corridor

Reported (not officially confirmed) ~400% growth in Kazakh/Kyrgyz/Uzbek dual-use re-exports toward Russia, plus the EU's 20th package tightening controls on the Kyrgyz corridor specifically, makes this a live, cross-border compliance risk rather than a single-country issue. **Operators** in banking, trade finance and logistics anywhere in the EAEU footprint face elevated counterparty and reputational screening burdens; **entrants** structuring regional supply chains should assume Western financial institutions will apply enhanced due diligence to any Kazakh/Kyrgyz/Uzbek trade-finance touchpoint.

**Net:** moderate-to-elevated and rising; the compliance cost of operating in the EAEU trade-finance space is now a distinct line item, separate from the underlying country risk of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan or Uzbekistan individually.

## Strategic Implications

**If you're already operating in the region:**
- Build in contingency for the Kazakhstan CPC export route being disrupted again while the Ukraine war continues — the July incident showed both the vulnerability (single-corridor dependence) and the recovery speed (10 days to resumption).
- Treat enhanced Western due-diligence scrutiny on any EAEU-domiciled trade-finance or banking relationship (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan) as an ongoing compliance cost, not a one-off — the EU's 20th sanctions package and re-export monitoring reports point the same direction.
- Plan energy-dependent operations in Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan around continued winter power-shortage risk: Kyrgyzstan's declared energy-sector emergency runs through December 2026, and Tajikistan's low Nurek reservoir level is already curbing power exports.

**If you're evaluating entry:**
- Uzbekistan is the standout near-term opportunity this month, particularly in critical minerals and processing capacity via the US-Uzbekistan Joint Investment Platform — the region's most concrete first-mover window identified in this edition.
- Middle Corridor logistics and rail infrastructure (the CKU railway, Kazakhstan Temir Zholy financing) offer a longer-horizon play, but treat the completion timeline as genuinely unresolved (sources disagree between 2028 and 2030) rather than anchoring on either estimate.
- Turkmenistan remains largely closed to non-Chinese entrants; treat its data opacity itself as a due-diligence red flag, not merely an information gap to work around.

**What to watch next month (September 2026):**
- The SCO's 25th-anniversary summit in Bishkek (31 Aug–1 Sept) — watch for institutional or financial reforms that could affect regional trade or investment rules.
- Whether Secretary Rubio's stated intent to visit all five Central Asian states in 2026 materializes — a concrete signal of follow-through on US commercial engagement.
- CPC/Tengiz export-route stability, given the war-related recurrence risk flagged above.
- Whether the Kambarata-1 intergovernmental construction agreement moves from talks to signature, and progress toward Rogun's third-unit target of September 2027.
